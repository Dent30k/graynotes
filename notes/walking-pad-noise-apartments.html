Walking-pad noise in apartments

Motor decibels versus the thump your downstairs neighbor actually hears.

August 21, 2026 · Chicago

Walking-pad listings love a decibel number from a motor on a test stand. Downstairs neighbors love none of that number. The sound that travels through a Chicago three-flat is not the whir. It is the thump: a 180-pound footfall on a six-inch deck on a wood joist. August 2026, windows open, someone home below you at 2 p.m. That is the test. If the pad only sounds like a quiet fan in your headphones, you have measured the wrong thing.

Airborne whir versus structure-borne thump

Motor noise is airborne. A brushless mill at 2 mph on a W50-class pad is a polite whir, often in the 40-something dB claims, which are taken at a distance and a load that are not your kitchen. You hear it. A partner on the couch hears it. A neighbor two floors down usually does not. Cheap pads with struggling rollers add a squeak and a belt slap that is still mostly airborne. Silicone on the belt, tracking the deck, tightening a shroud — those help the whir. They do not help the neighbor.

Thump is structure-borne. Energy goes into the floor, into the joists, into the light fixture below. Speed makes it worse. Heel-striking makes it worse. A pad with a dead deck makes it worse because the frame becomes a drum. Incline can make it worse if the rear feet become two small pressure points. A 4 mph walk is a different instrument than a 2 mph walk. If you live above anyone, 4 mph is a courtesy question, not a spec question. The buyer’s-guide 4 mph test is about motors. This note is about buildings.

Buildings, hours, and rugs

Concrete slab is the kind pad. Wood joists in a 1920s two-flat are the unkind pad. Vinyl over plywood over sleepers is a snare drum. A thick rug under the pad helps a little and can hide a wandering belt. A dedicated rubber appliance mat helps more, especially one larger than the frame so the feet do not stand on the rug’s pile and rock. A mat that is too small concentrates load. A mat that is too soft becomes a trampoline and adds bounce to the keyboard. Try the firm mat first.

Hours matter as much as materials. 6:30 a.m. on a workday is a different letter under the door than 11 a.m. 9 p.m. is worse than both in a building with kids. If you must walk early, stay at 1.5–2.0 mph and keep the incline at zero. If you want 3.5 mph, do it when the building is a building, not a bedroom. This is not a moral. It is how apartments work. Homeowners on a slab can ignore most of this paragraph. Renters who just installed LVP on a floated floor cannot.

What you can actually change

Gait: shorter steps, quieter heels, shoes with a little foam, not dress socks on a slick belt. Speed: cap the working preset at 2.2 mph even if the motor will do 4. Placement: not in the middle of a long joist span if you can help it; nearer a wall or over a beam is quieter below. Maintenance: a dry belt slaps. A mis-tracked belt rubs the shroud. Both sound like failure and both travel. The belt-care note is the rest of that sentence.

A “silent” listing is a motor claim. Ask, if you can, the person below you after a twenty-minute walk at working speed, not after a thirty-second demo. If you cannot ask, assume wood plus 3 mph plus morning is a no. Cheap 40 × 100 cm pads fail this test twice: they thump because the deck is a tray, and they squeal because the motor is a toy. The Superfit-class 2-in-1 adds handlebar rattle, which is airborne and irritating in the same room, less relevant downstairs. The W50’s 400 lb frame is stiffer than a $90 pad. Stiffer is quieter in the floor. It is not silent.

Headphones are not a building

Noise-cancelling headphones will convince you the pad is polite. They will not convince a stroller on the ceiling of the apartment below. If you already fight with a washer on spin, you already know your joists. Put the pad where the washer would be if you had a choice, not where the rug looks good. US apartments, US hours. The decibel badge can stay on the listing. Your neighbor is the spec.

If the building is a problem, the pad is not the only tool. Walk outside. Use the pad at noon. Stand still for calls. A walking pad that you use one quiet hour a day is a better object than a walking pad you use badly from 6 a.m. to 8 p.m. because a spec sheet said 4 mph. Courtesy is a setting. It does not come from Merach. It comes from the floor.

Calls, dogs, and the same-room problem

The downstairs neighbor is one audience. The person on the Zoom is another. A whir that is fine in the room becomes a wind tunnel in a laptop mic at 2 mph. Mute more. Use a headset. Do not put the laptop on the desk’s resonance node — usually the center of a wide top — if you can put it on a side return. Dogs respond to belt noise like they respond to vacuum cleaners: not well, then too well. A closed door is a courtesy to the dog and to the meeting.

Same-room partners hear belt slap as conversation. If someone is reading ten feet away, 3 mph is a different house than 1.8. Agree on hours the way you agree on a blender. The pad is a blender that runs for forty minutes. That comparison is unkind and accurate.

If you are on a slab in a 2019 building, most of this note is optional and the motor claim is closer to the truth. If you are in a vintage walk-up with plaster and a neighbor who works nights, the motor claim is a distraction. Buy the stiffest deck you were going to buy anyway, put a firm mat under it, walk at typing speed, and keep 4 mph for hours when the building can take it. That is the whole apartment guide. The listing’s dB number can remain a listing’s dB number.

One more diagnostic: record a video with the phone on the floor of the apartment below, if you have access, or on your own floor next to the pad and then on a windowsill in the unit underneath a shared wall. You are listening for thud, not for motor. If the thud is the story, no lubricant will save you. If the motor is the story, maintenance might. People oil a belt to fix a neighbor and then wonder why the joists did not read the instruction sheet. Different problems. Different tools. August windows make both louder. Winter carpets make both kinder. Do not wait for January to find out you live above a night-shift nurse.
